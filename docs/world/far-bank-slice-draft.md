## Purpose

The first route ends with an unanswered relay post and an optional report of two calls near the river. This route lets the player meet people across the water, learn why a crossing matters day to day, and compare two observations before filing another report. Its local problem is a route safety decision. It does not reveal the cause of Hollowtide or decide what happened in any real flood.

The account Preston remembers about St. Joseph and Elwood is a reason to write the far bank as a community with its own knowledge and priorities. The fictional route must not present an intentional real-world levee break as a verified fact. Do not stage a forced “save one town or the other” choice on this first visit.

## Entry from either opening report

- If the player reported both calls, the dispatcher says a second runner heard an uncertain answer and asks the player to record the time and direction of any repeat.
- If the player reported only the empty post, a routine route check finds the same missing return mark. The dispatcher asks for a crossing inspection. The river clue remains discoverable.
- Both routes lead to the same work assignment. Reporting an uncertainty should change the conversation, not lock the player out of the chapter.

## The walkable loop

1. **Crossing approach.** A small working crossing carries the player from the old landing to the far bank. A Wayline board identifies the last *confirmed* crossing condition and when it was checked. This teaches that a route mark can expire.
2. **Far-bank repair point.** A route maintainer is moving supplies to a raised track. They explain which path residents currently use and ask the player to carry a replacement marker and observe two possible approaches. They speak for their own work, not for every resident.
3. **Raised track.** The player sees a usable path and a high-water stain on a built surface. The mark records past water, not an explanation of who caused it. A resident can describe the inconvenience of a detour in plain terms.
4. **Lower cut.** The short path has fresh erosion and creature tracks. The player can mark it “check before crossing,” without declaring it permanently unsafe. A visible Tide creature belongs here after its approved art is ready; the encounter is optional.
5. **Two listening points.** From the raised track and the lower cut, the player can hear a similar two-note pattern at different strengths. Field Notes record place and order of hearing. They do not label the source a creature, echo, or signal.
6. **Return report.** The maintainer and dispatcher accept a temporary route warning and the player’s observations. If the player heard both points, the report can distinguish them. If not, it records only what was checked. The safe route remains provisional.

The return consequence is small but visible: the Wayline board gains a dated warning, and an NPC uses the route the player marked for inspection. The player has helped someone travel today while the larger mystery remains open.

## Writing guardrails

Use “I saw,” “I heard,” and “checked at this time” for the player’s reports. Give residents distinct tasks and opinions; do not make one person the voice of a whole community. A visible flood mark is evidence of water height, not proof of a deliberate act. The first visit may explain the keeper’s ordinary absence, but should leave the two-note call and missing return mark open if the player has not gathered enough evidence.
