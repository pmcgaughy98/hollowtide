I am designing Hollowtide, a 2D pixel-art monster-taming RPG (Godot) set along the Missouri River around St. Joseph, Missouri. Creatures are shown in battle with a front view (opponent) and a back view (player's side), with idle animations and cinematic "signature" moves. I want to study how other monster-taming games solved these problems, especially games that are discontinued, delisted, shut down, or hard to find.

Honesty rule: only report sources, URLs, games and facts you can verify. If you cannot confirm something (a URL, whether a game is shut down, a license), label it UNVERIFIED instead of guessing. Do not invent games, links or dates.

Research and report:

1. ARCHIVES. Where can a designer legally view or download reference material (sprite rips, battle screenshots, longplay videos, design documents, unused content) for monster-taming games, including dead ones? Cover at least: The Spriters Resource, Internet Archive (Wayback Machine and its software and video collections), Flashpoint (Flash and browser games), The Cutting Room Floor, Lost Media Wiki, MobyGames, game Fandom wikis, and YouTube longplay channels. For each: what it holds, how complete it is for this genre, how to search it, and any terms of use.

2. GAMES TO STUDY. List 20 to 30 monster-taming or creature-battle games worth studying, prioritising discontinued or delisted ones (shut-down mobile and browser games, Japan-only handheld games, old console titles), but include a few influential live ones (for example Pokemon Gen 3 to 5, Dragon Quest Monsters, Telefang, Coromon, Cassette Beasts, Monster Rancher). For each: platform, years active, current status (live, delisted, shut down; with source), art style and sprite size, and what specifically is worth studying.

3. SPECIFIC PROBLEMS. For each, find concrete examples with sources:
   a. Back-view sprites: how games drew the player's creature from behind, how they kept it matching the front, and how much of it the UI hides.
   b. Idle animation: frame counts and techniques (breathing, part sway, 2-frame vs many-frame loops) at different sprite sizes.
   c. Big "ultimate" or signature move presentation: camera, backgrounds, cut-in title cards, timing.
   d. Evolution design: how later stages read as evolved rather than just bigger.
   e. Battle-screen layout: text box, HP bars, and sprite placement.

4. LEGAL LINE. Explain plainly, for a small indie team in the United States: what counts as acceptable reference and study, and what crosses into infringement (tracing, recolouring, ripping assets into the game). Cover "abandonware" (is a discontinued game's art still copyrighted?), the fan-game takedowns Nintendo and others have issued, and whether using ripped sprites as reference images for AI image generators creates legal or ethical risk. Cite sources; this is not legal advice.

Output format: an executive summary of 10 bullet points or fewer; a table of archives (name, URL, contents, best use, terms); a table of games (name, platform, years, status, why study it, best archive for it); a section per problem in part 3 with examples; the legal section; and a list of everything you marked UNVERIFIED.
